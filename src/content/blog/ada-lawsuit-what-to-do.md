If you just got an **ADA lawsuit** over your website, the first week matters more than any other. This guide walks you through what to do next, in order, from the moment you are served to the point where you and your lawyer pick a strategy. It is written for small and mid-size business owners, especially online stores, which are the most common targets.

*This is general information, not legal advice. Every case is different, and you need a lawyer who defends ADA cases.*

## First, Confirm It Is Really an ADA Lawsuit

An ADA lawsuit comes as a **summons and complaint** filed with a court. It names the court, a case number and you (or your company) as the defendant. If what you received is a letter from a law firm asking for money but with no case number, it is a demand letter, not a lawsuit yet. Read our guide to [what to do after an ADA demand letter](/blog/ada-demand-letter) instead.

Most website cases are brought under Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act, which covers businesses open to the public. Our guide to [ADA Title III law for businesses](/blog/ada-title-iii-law-for-businesses) explains who it covers.

### Step 1: Write Down the Date You Were Served

In federal court you have **21 days** after being served to file an answer or a motion, under [Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_12). If you agreed to waive formal service, you get **60 days** from the date the waiver request was sent. State courts have their own deadlines. Missing the deadline can lead to a default judgment, so put the date on your calendar before you do anything else.

### Step 2: Call a Lawyer Who Defends ADA Cases

This is the most important step. A lawyer who regularly defends ADA website cases knows the plaintiff firms, what similar cases settled for in your court, and which defenses work there. Do not contact the plaintiff or their lawyer yourself, and do not post about the lawsuit.

### Step 3: Tell Your Insurance Company

Some business insurance policies cover part of the defense costs of an ADA lawsuit, and many do not. Send the complaint to your broker or insurer right away and ask whether it is covered. Late notice can affect coverage, so do not wait until you have decided what to do.

### Step 4: Preserve Your Records

Do not delete pages, change hosting or wipe analytics to make the problem disappear. Keep a copy of the site as it was, along with any accessibility work you have already done, such as earlier audits, bug tickets or fixes. If you have been running scans, export them. A dated history of real accessibility work helps your lawyer. See what goes into an [ADA compliance report for your lawyer](/blog/ada-compliance-report-for-lawyer).

### Step 5: Scan Your Website for the Issues in the Complaint

Most complaints list specific barriers: images with no alt text, form fields with no labels, buttons a screen reader cannot name, or a checkout that cannot be used with a keyboard. Run the [free ADA compliance checker](/tools/ada-compliance-checker) on the pages named in the complaint, your home page and your key templates, such as a product page and checkout. You get a list of the issues automated testing can find, ranked by severity, against WCAG 2.1 Level AA, the benchmark used in ADA website cases.

Automated testing does not cover everything. Our guide to [automated vs. manual accessibility testing](/blog/automated-vs-manual-accessibility-testing) covers the manual checks, such as using the site with only a keyboard.

### Step 6: Look Up the Plaintiff's Other Lawsuits

Many website cases are filed by the same plaintiffs and firms against dozens of businesses. You can search federal dockets on [PACER](https://pacer.uscourts.gov/) or the free [CourtListener](https://www.courtlistener.com/) database. Give what you find to your lawyer. Courts have started to look harder at serial filings: in February 2026, Judge Jeannette Vargas in the Southern District of New York ordered discovery and a hearing on whether the plaintiff in *Fernandez v. Cuddle Clones* had really tried to buy from the site, after noting more than 57 similar suits ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/02/ny-judge-questions-plaintiffs-pet-plushie-purchase-tale-orders-jurisdictional-discovery-in-website-access-suit/)).

### Step 7: Start Fixing the Website, and Keep a Dated Record

Fixing the barriers is the one step that helps in every outcome. It lowers the cost of a settlement, it can support a mootness defense, and it stops the next lawsuit, because businesses that have been sued once are often sued again.

Start with whatever blocks someone from finishing a task: missing labels in checkout, unnamed buttons, images without [alt text](/resources/wcag/1-1-1-non-text-content) and low [color contrast](/resources/wcag/1-4-3-contrast-minimum). Keep a dated log of every fix. Our guide on how to [fix your website after an ADA lawsuit](/blog/fix-website-after-ada-lawsuit) covers the order and the code.

### Step 8: Decide Your Strategy With Your Lawyer

By the end of the first week you and your lawyer can choose a path. The usual options are:

- **Settle early.** Often the cheapest route, especially before discovery starts. Expect the settlement to include a commitment to fix the site by a deadline.
- **Fix the site and argue the claim is moot.** Federal Title III claims ask for the barriers to be fixed. If you can prove they are fixed and will stay fixed, a court can dismiss the claim. In March 2026, Judge J. Paul Oetken dismissed *Jones v. Moscot* in the Southern District of New York on that basis, relying on a declaration and an accessibility vendor's report that the plaintiff did not dispute ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/04/lawsuit-dismissed-as-moot-based-on-unrebutted-evidence-that-website-is-accessible/)). The bar is high: the court required it to be "absolutely clear" the site would stay accessible.
- **Challenge standing or jurisdiction.** If the plaintiff never really tried to use your site, or your business has no connection to the court where you were sued, your lawyer may move to dismiss.

Our guide to the [ADA lawsuit process](/blog/ada-lawsuit-process) explains what happens at each stage after this, and [how long an ADA lawsuit takes](/blog/how-long-does-an-ada-lawsuit-take) covers the timeline.

## What Not To Do After an ADA Lawsuit

- **Do not ignore it.** A default judgment is usually worse than any settlement.
- **Do not install an overlay widget and call it fixed.** Overlays do not change your code. In 2025 the Federal Trade Commission ordered overlay vendor accessiBe to pay $1 million over misleading claims about its product ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2025/05/federal-trade-commission-orders-accessibe-to-pay-1m-for-misleading-claims-relating-to-automated-website-accessibility-remediation-tool/)). See [accessiBe vs. AccessBell](/comparisons/accessibe-vs-accessbell).
- **Do not promise anything in writing** to the plaintiff without your lawyer.

## If Your Business Is in Missouri, California or New York

Where you were sued changes the rules:

- **Missouri** passed a law in May 2026, effective August 28, 2026, that lets a Missouri defendant sue over an abusive website accessibility lawsuit and recover fees ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/05/missouri-passes-law-to-deter-abusive-website-litigation/)).
- **California** lets plaintiffs seek statutory damages under the Unruh Act on top of the federal claim. See [California website accessibility law](/state-accessibility-laws/california).
- **New York** federal courts have tightened standing for repeat plaintiffs, which has pushed some cases into state court. See [New York website accessibility law](/state-accessibility-laws/new-york).
