**ADA lawsuit payouts** get a lot of attention, and a lot of the numbers floating around online have no source. This guide looks at five real website ADA lawsuits where the amounts are public, explains who was paid and why, and covers what drives the cost of a settlement for a business that is sued.

*This is general information, not legal advice. A lawyer who defends ADA cases can tell you what similar cases settle for in your court.*

## How ADA Lawsuit Payouts Work

Before the cases, one point explains most of the numbers. Under Title III of the ADA, which covers businesses, a private plaintiff can ask a court for two things: an order to fix the barriers, and attorney fees ([42 U.S.C. § 12188](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12188)). There are no money damages for the plaintiff under the federal law.

So where does the money in an ADA lawsuit payout come from? Three places:

- **Attorney fees.** Often the biggest part. The business pays the plaintiff's lawyers, on top of its own.
- **State-law damages.** California's Unruh Civil Rights Act allows at least $4,000 per violation ([Cal. Civ. Code § 52](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=52.)), which is why so many cases are filed there. See [California website accessibility law](/state-accessibility-laws/california).
- **Civil penalties.** When the Department of Justice brings or joins a case, it can seek civil penalties paid to the government.

And in every case, the business also pays to fix the website, usually under a deadline and with outside monitoring.

### 1. NFB v. Target: About $9.74 Million

The National Federation of the Blind sued Target in 2006 because blind shoppers could not use its website with screen readers. The class action settled in August 2008. Target set up a **$6 million** fund for California class members, with up to $7,000 per claimant, paid **$20,000** to the California Center for the Blind, and paid **$3,738,864.96** in the plaintiffs' attorney fees and costs. It agreed to make the site accessible by early 2009 and to three years of monitoring by the NFB, without admitting it broke the law ([W3C WAI case study](https://www.w3.org/WAI/business-case/archive/target-case-study)).

**Why it was so large:** it was a class action under California law, where each class member could claim statutory damages, and it was fought for more than two years.

### 2. Alcazar v. Fashion Nova: $5.15 Million Proposed

A class action against clothing retailer Fashion Nova, filed in 2020 in the Northern District of California. The parties proposed a **$5.15 million** settlement, with payments of up to $4,000 per California class member under the Unruh Act. In February 2026 the Department of Justice opposed it, saying it had no concrete commitment to fix the website, no monitoring and no enforcement, and that the attorney fees were out of proportion ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/02/doj-throws-wrench-into-proposed-ada-website-accessibility-class-settlement/)). It had not been finally approved when this was written.

**Why it matters:** a big payout without a real fix is now being challenged. Paying money does not replace fixing the site. More on this case in our roundup of [2026 ADA lawsuits](/blog/ada-lawsuits-2026).

### 3. NAD v. Netflix: $795,000

The National Association of the Deaf sued Netflix in 2011 over missing captions on streamed movies and shows. Under the October 2012 consent decree, Netflix paid **$755,000** in attorney fees and costs and **$40,000** toward monitoring, and agreed to caption 100% of its streamed content by September 30, 2014 ([consent decree, via DREDF](https://dredf.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/netflix-consent-decree.pdf)).

**Why it was smaller:** it was not a damages class action, so almost all of the money went to fees. The bigger cost for Netflix was the captioning itself.

### 4. U.S. v. H&R Block: $100,000

The Department of Justice joined a lawsuit against H&R Block in December 2013 over its website, online tax software and mobile apps. Under the 2014 consent decree, H&R Block paid **$45,000** to the two individual plaintiffs and a **$55,000** civil penalty, and agreed to make its site and apps conform to WCAG 2.0 Level AA, under a five-year decree ([Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-reaches-settlement-hr-block-ensure-tax-services-are-accessible-individuals)).

**Why it matters:** this is what the DOJ's involvement looks like. A civil penalty is possible, and a named technical standard, WCAG, goes into the agreement.

### 5. Robles v. Domino's: Not Disclosed

Guillermo Robles, who is blind, sued Domino's in 2016 after being unable to order on its website and app with a screen reader. The case went to the Ninth Circuit and the Supreme Court declined to hear Domino's appeal in 2019. The parties settled in June 2022 on confidential terms.

**Why it matters:** many ADA lawsuit payouts are never made public, especially for small businesses. Be wary of any article that quotes a precise "average ADA settlement" without a source. Read [how long an ADA lawsuit takes](/blog/how-long-does-an-ada-lawsuit-take) to see how the Domino's case stretched to nearly six years.

## What Drives the Size of an ADA Lawsuit Payout

From these five ADA lawsuit payouts, a few factors stand out:

1. **Where you are sued.** States with damages laws, above all California, raise the stakes.
2. **Class action or single plaintiff.** Damages multiply across a class. Most small-business cases are single plaintiffs, which keeps the number much lower.
3. **How long it is fought.** Every month of litigation adds attorney fees on both sides.
4. **Whether the DOJ is involved.** That can add civil penalties.
5. **Whether the site gets fixed.** A real fix gives your lawyer something to offer and, as in *Jones v. Moscot* in 2026, can even end the federal claim.

The payouts above are also not the full cost. The business pays its own lawyers, the cost of fixing the site, and often the cost of monitoring.
