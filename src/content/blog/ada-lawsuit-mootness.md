**ADA lawsuit mootness** is the rule that lets a court end a federal ADA Title III claim when there is nothing left to order: the website is already accessible, and it is "absolutely clear" the barriers will not come back. It is one of the few ways a fix can end a case instead of just lowering a settlement. This page explains when it works, what it does not end, and the evidence it takes. For where mootness fits among the other stages, read the [stages of the ADA lawsuit process](/blog/ada-lawsuit-process).

<li>Private Title III plaintiffs get the remedies of 42 U.S.C. § 2000a-3(a): "preventive relief," meaning an injunction, plus attorney fees. No money damages under the federal ADA (<a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12188">42 U.S.C. § 12188(a)(1)</a>).</li>

<li>Mootness does not erase state-law damages, such as at least $4,000 per violation under California's Unruh Act (<a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=52.">Cal. Civ. Code § 52</a>).</li>

## What ADA Lawsuit Mootness Means

Federal courts can only decide live disputes. If something happens after a lawsuit is filed that leaves the court nothing to order, the claim is "moot," and the court dismisses it for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Defendants usually raise this in a motion under [Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/frcp/rule_12), or later in the case.

For a website ADA lawsuit, the question is simple to state and hard to prove: is the site accessible now, and will it stay that way?

## Why Website ADA Lawsuits Can Become Moot

A Title III lawsuit brought by a private person can win only forward-looking relief. [42 U.S.C. § 12188(a)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12188) gives private plaintiffs the remedies in [42 U.S.C. § 2000a-3(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/2000a-3), which describes "a civil action for preventive relief, including an application for a permanent or temporary injunction." The ADA adds that injunctive relief can include an order to alter facilities, provide an auxiliary aid or service, or modify a policy.

So the plaintiff is asking the court to order the barriers removed. If they are already removed for good, that order has no work left to do. This is why mootness comes up in website cases far more than in cases about money damages.

## The "Absolutely Clear" Test for ADA Lawsuit Mootness

Fixing the website after you are sued is "voluntary cessation," and courts are suspicious of it, because a business could fix a page, win a dismissal and then let the barriers come back. Three Supreme Court decisions set the bar:

| Case | What the Court said | What it means for a website fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw (2000)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/98-822) | A case is moot only if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur." | Proving the site is accessible today is not enough. You also have to show it will not slide back. |
| [Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc. (2013)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/11-982) | A defendant "cannot automatically moot a case simply by ending its unlawful conduct once sued," and bears a "formidable burden." | The business, not the plaintiff, has to prove the fix holds. |
| [FBI v. Fikre (2024)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-1178) | Voluntary cessation moots a case "only if the defendant can show that the practice cannot 'reasonably be expected to recur.'" | The 2024 Court restated the same demanding standard. It has not been relaxed. |

In practice, courts look at whether the barriers named in the complaint are gone, whether someone independent checked, and whether the business has a real process that keeps new barriers out. A website changes every time someone adds a product, a page or a plugin, so a promise alone rarely carries the burden.

## Jones v. Moscot: An ADA Lawsuit Dismissed as Moot After a Website Fix

The clearest recent example is *Jones v. Moscot.com, LLC*, No. 25-cv-1843, in the Southern District of New York. The eyewear retailer fixed its website and filed a sworn declaration from an e-commerce executive and a report from accessibility firm Level Access. The plaintiff did not offer evidence to dispute them.

On March 5, 2026, Judge J. Paul Oetken dismissed the federal ADA claim as moot. The dismissal was without prejudice, and the state-law claim was left for state court ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/04/lawsuit-dismissed-as-moot-based-on-unrebutted-evidence-that-website-is-accessible/)). Our [2026 ADA lawsuits roundup](/blog/ada-lawsuits-2026) puts the case next to the year's other rulings, and [how long an ADA lawsuit takes](/blog/how-long-does-an-ada-lawsuit-take) shows it was among the quickest website cases to end.

Two lessons stand out. The evidence was specific and independent, and it went unanswered. A business that files a vague statement that the site "is being improved" is in a much weaker spot.

## What ADA Lawsuit Mootness Does Not End

A moot federal claim is a real win, but it is narrower than it sounds:

- **State-law claims.** Laws like California's Unruh Act award damages for past violations (at least $4,000 each under [Cal. Civ. Code § 52](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=52.)). Fixing the site does not undo a past visit. When the federal claim is gone, the court may decline to keep the state claims under [28 U.S.C. § 1367(c)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1367), and the plaintiff can refile them in state court, as happened in *Moscot*.
- **Department of Justice cases.** When the Attorney General sues under [42 U.S.C. § 12188(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12188), the court may award monetary damages to people harmed and a civil penalty, which a later fix does not erase. The statute does tell the court to consider good-faith efforts to comply.
- **The next plaintiff.** Mootness ends one case. If the barriers come back, anyone can sue again.

**Attorney fees work differently.** The ADA lets a court award fees to the "prevailing party" ([42 U.S.C. § 12205](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12205)). In [Buckhannon v. West Virginia DHHR (2001)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/99-1848), the Supreme Court held the "catalyst theory" is "not a permissible basis for the award of attorney's fees under the FHAA and ADA." A plaintiff whose federal claim is dismissed as moot because you fixed the site has not prevailed in that sense. State fee rules can differ, so ask your lawyer.

**A different kind of mootness.** In [Acheson Hotels v. Laufer (2023)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-429), the Supreme Court vacated a tester case as moot because the plaintiff voluntarily dismissed her pending suits, not because the hotel fixed anything. That is why the Court never answered whether testers have standing.

## Evidence to Gather Before Arguing an ADA Lawsuit Is Moot

Your lawyer decides what to file. This is the evidence that tends to matter in website cases, based on what the *Moscot* court relied on and what the "absolutely clear" test asks for:

| Evidence | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| A list of every barrier named in the complaint, with the page and the date each was fixed | Shows the specific barriers the plaintiff sued over are gone. |
| An independent accessibility audit after the fixes, against WCAG 2.1 or 2.2 Level AA | *Moscot* relied on a report from an outside accessibility firm. |
| A sworn declaration from someone who runs the website | Ties the fixes and the ongoing process to a person who can be held to them. |
| Dated before-and-after scan results | Shows when each issue appeared and when it was cleared. |
| A written accessibility policy and a named owner | Shows the fix is part of how the site is run, not a one-off. |
| A schedule of ongoing scans and manual checks, with results | Speaks directly to whether barriers "could not reasonably be expected to recur." |
| A plan for third-party content (chat widgets, booking tools, payment pages) | These are common sources of new barriers after a fix. |

Automated scans catch only part of WCAG. A keyboard and screen reader check by a person belongs in the audit.
